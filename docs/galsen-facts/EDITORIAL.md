# Editorial standard

The goal is to make public information easier to understand without hiding uncertainty, source limits or time periods.

## Every exploration should answer

1. What is the question?
2. What sources are used?
3. What period and population do they cover?
4. What does the material actually show?
5. What does it not allow us to conclude?
6. What is the clearest visual or comparison for the reader?
7. Can the underlying cleaned data be shared?

## Recommended page anatomy

- headline framed as a clear question or finding;
- short context paragraph;
- 2–5 key figures;
- one main chart or infographic before adding secondary visuals;
- interpretation in plain language;
- methodology/limitations box;
- source list with direct links and covered periods;
- update/retrieval date;
- optional downloadable data.

## Time discipline

Historical material must always be labeled with its actual period. Never present an older dataset as a description of the present.

For current claims, record the retrieval/update date and prefer primary public sources where possible.

## Visual discipline

Graphics should clarify a point, not decorate it. Use the project colors sparingly and preserve readable contrast. Red is not automatically negative and green is not automatically positive; legends must explain meaning.

## Voice

Clear, calm, concrete and accessible. Avoid institutional jargon, sensational framing and claims stronger than the evidence.
